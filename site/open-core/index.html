Open core, and where the line sits

Anything a single team needs on a single cluster is Apache-2.0 and stays that way. Anything about many teams, many clusters, auditors, and retention is where a paid product would live.

That is the whole rule. It is stated here before there is anything to sell, because a line drawn after a project has users is a line drawn against them.

Free, forever, Apache-2.0

The entire tool described on this site — reconciliation, drift detection, in-use CVE prioritization, debloat recommendations, signing and verifying runtime attestations, the Kubernetes deployment, and the output schemas — is the open-source core. Concretely, that means:

  • You can run cruthu against your workloads, gate your CI on its exit code, and produce and verify signed runtime attestations without paying anyone or registering anything.
  • The attestation predicate is published as a versioned, standalone spec, so the evidence cruthu emits is verifiable with cosign alone. If this project disappeared tomorrow, artifacts already signed would remain checkable.
  • There is no license key, no phone-home, no telemetry, and no feature that degrades unless you buy something.

What a paid layer would be

None of this exists yet. It sits at 2.0 in the roadmap, behind a 0.x series that is still being written, and it will be shaped by what real usage turns out to justify. Today it is a direction, not a product:

  • Fleet view. One pane across clusters — conformance status per workload, drift timeline, attestation coverage.
  • Policy engine. Org-wide rules ("prod namespaces require a conformant attestation less than seven days old"), distributed to admission controllers.
  • Compliance packs. Mapped evidence bundles for EO 14028, the EU CRA, SOC 2 change-management evidence, FedRAMP continuous-monitoring inputs.
  • Attestation ledger. Retained, searchable history of signed runtime attestations — the compliance artifact as a service.
  • Hosting and a managed backend for the above, with a single-tenant deployment option, because the buyers who care most about this are definitionally the ones least willing to ship their runtime inventory to a multi-tenant service.

The pattern in that list is deliberate. Every item is about coordination across boundaries — teams, clusters, time, auditors. None of them is a capability withheld from the tool itself.

Why draw it there

Because it is a line that stays legible under pressure. A vendor who reserves "the good detections" for the paid tier has an incentive to make the free tier miss things, and in a drift detector, missing things is the primary failure mode. Keeping the detector whole and charging for fleet-scale coordination puts the incentive in the right place: the open core has to be genuinely good, because it is what everyone evaluates and most people will keep using.

It is also the honest read of where the work is. Retention, multi-tenancy, and auditor-shaped evidence packs are real, ongoing operational cost. Detecting that a binary ran which nobody declared is not.

Practicalities

The core is Apache-2.0, which is CNCF-compatible and imposes nothing on you. The license for a future control plane — permissive-adjacent BSL versus plain proprietary — is an open question to be settled at 1.0, and deliberately not before; license anxiety is a poor reason to slow down a tool that does not have its first release yet.

This site and the module path are served from cruthu.dev. If a hosted product happens, it gets its own subdomains rather than this one, so the origin that answers Go module resolution never grows an authenticated application or a session cookie.

Interested in the paid layer?

There is no waitlist, on purpose. The control plane is far enough out that an address collected today would go stale before there was anything to send to it, and a project whose pitch is careful handling of other people's data should not start by accumulating email it cannot use.

If any of it sounds like something you would actually deploy — especially if your interest is the compliance evidence rather than the dashboard — open an issue on GitHub. Being told which parts matter now is considerably more useful than a list of addresses.
